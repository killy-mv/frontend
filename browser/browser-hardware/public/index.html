Reach hardware

A page can open your camera, read your GPS, talk to a USB device and flash a microcontroller. What stops it is not a missing API — it is three gates it has to pass first. Panel 1 is those gates; panels 2 to 5 are what lies behind them.

Most of this needs actual hardware. Every panel degrades honestly: if the API is missing or the device is not there, the log says which of the two it was. Start with What will this browser let me touch? — it tells you which of the panels below are worth pressing in the browser you are using right now.

1 The three gates

Every powerful capability is gated by a secure context (HTTPS, or localhost), a user gesture, and an explicit permission — and on top of that a Permissions-Policy that the containing document controls. The design stance is that a page is untrusted code from a stranger, so nothing is assumed and everything is earned. In practice the permission UX, not the API, is what decides whether an idea ships at all.

How to see it
  1. Press What will this browser let me touch?. Then open the same page in Firefox or Safari and compare. The bottom half of that list is where the engines part company.
  2. Press Ask for the screen (from your click) — the picker appears. Then press Ask 1 second after the click and watch it fail without ever showing UI. The call is identical; only the fact that a click was in progress changed.
  3. Press What am I already allowed? to read permission states without triggering a single prompt. Grant the camera in panel 2 and press it again.
  4. Press Load the three frames below. All three run byte-identical code from the same server. Press Try the camera in each and read the verdicts.
  5. Open /locked/ — the same file again, served with Permissions-Policy: camera=(), geolocation=(). It is a top-level page you navigated to yourself, and it still cannot ask. A header took the capability away from the whole document.


        

          
          
          
        

      


      
      

        
2 Camera and microphone

        

          The one capability every engine ships, and the one where the privacy design is easiest to
          watch working. Two things to look for: device labels are blank until you grant
          access, because a list of your hardware is a fingerprint; and what you ask for in
          getUserMedia constraints is a request, not a command — the camera answers with
          what it actually has.
        


        

          How to see it
          
    
            
  1. Press List devices before granting anything. You get the right number of entries with empty label fields — enough to know a camera exists, not enough to know which one.
    2. 
            
  2. Press Turn on the camera, accept the prompt, then press List devices again. The names appeared. That is the trade the prompt bought.
    3. 
            
  3. Press What did I actually get? and compare getSettings() with getCapabilities() — what you were given against what the hardware can do.
    4. 
            
  4. Press Ask for 4K. Unless you own a 4K webcam you will be handed something else and no error is raised. Constraints negotiate; only exact: ones fail loudly.
    5. 
            
  5. Press Turn on the microphone and speak — the meter moves. Then press Turn everything off and watch the camera light on your machine go out. Forgetting track.stop() is the reason so many sites leave that light on.
    6. 
            
  6. On a phone, Flip camera switches to the front lens by re-requesting with facingMode.

3 The screen itself

        

          getDisplayMedia returns a video track of a monitor, a window or a tab. The
          interesting part is what the page is not allowed to do: it cannot enumerate your
          windows, cannot preselect one, cannot style the picker, and cannot stop the browser from
          showing a "you are sharing" bar. Every screen-share tool you have used is this API plus a
          WebRTC connection.
        


        

          How to see it
          
    
            
  1. Press Share a screen or window. The picker is drawn by the browser, outside the page — no site can restyle or fake it.
    2. 
            
  2. Press What am I sharing?. You get displaySurface: monitor, window or browser. That is roughly all the page learns.
    3. 
            
  3. Press Grab a still to pull a frame into a canvas. The pixels are ordinary ImageData from there on.
    4. 
            
  4. Now press the browser's own Stop sharing button rather than the one on this page. The log reports it: the track fired ended and the page found out afterwards. The user is in charge of ending it, not the site.

4 Sensors and input

        

          The rest of what the machine knows about itself. These are worth grouping because their
          permission models are all different: geolocation prompts, orientation is free on desktop
          but gated behind a gesture on iOS, the gamepad is hidden until you press a button on it,
          and vibration silently does nothing rather than failing. Half of this panel only comes
          alive on a phone — the server printed a LAN URL for that.
        


        

          How to see it
          
    
            
  1. Press Where am I?. Look at accuracy: tens of metres means GPS, hundreds or thousands means it guessed from your IP or nearby Wi-Fi. Then press Watch me move — the callback fires again whenever the estimate improves.
    2. 
            
  2. Press Start orientation and tilt the device. On a laptop with no sensors nothing arrives, and the log says so rather than pretending.
    3. 
            
  3. Plug in a controller and press Find gamepads. It reports nothing until you press a button on the pad — the browser hides it until you prove it is yours. Then the readout updates via requestAnimationFrame, because there are no button events; the Gamepad API is poll-only.
    4. 
            
  4. Press Vibrate on a phone. On a desktop it returns without doing anything and without throwing, which is its own lesson about feature detection.
    5. 
            
  5. Press Battery. It works in Chromium and was removed from Firefox and Safari for fingerprinting — a capability that existed and was taken back.

5 Devices, directly

        

          The Chromium-only tier: USB, serial ports, HID and Bluetooth, addressed from a web page.
          The pattern is identical for all four and worth learning once — requestDevice()
          takes filters, the browser runs the enumeration, and the page receives back exactly
          the one device the user picked. A site can never list your hardware, which is precisely the
          part a native program can. This is the boundary that makes "flash firmware from a web page"
          acceptable at all.
        


        

          How to see it
          
    
            
  1. Press any Ask for… button. Even with nothing plugged in, the picker appears — that dialog is the permission. There is no separate prompt.
    2. 
            
  2. Cancel it. The page gets NotFoundError, exactly the same error it gets when no device exists. The site cannot tell "the user refused" from "there was nothing there", and that is deliberate.
    3. 
            
  3. Pick something real, then press What was I granted?. Grants persist across reloads per origin and device, so a tool does not re-prompt every visit. Chrome lists them under Settings → Privacy → Site settings → USB devices.
    4. 
            
  4. In Firefox or Safari the buttons report a missing API. That is not an oversight — those engines treat this tier as a fingerprinting and safety risk and decline to ship it.

          The through-line: none of these APIs are dangerous on their own, and all of them are
          dangerous without the gates. When a capability is missing in Safari or Firefox it is
          usually not unimplemented but declined — so "the browser can do X" often means
          "Chrome can do X", and checking that is part of the work.
